<p>I am an applied environmental economist and a PhD candidate in the <a href="https://www.econ.utah.edu/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Department of Economics at the University of Utah</a>, currently on the 2026–2027 job market <a href="/research/">[Job Market Paper]</a> <a href="/cv/">[CV]</a>. My research studies how the design of environmental policy, and the markets that deliver it, shape who benefits. More broadly, my interests lie in how the effects of environmental change are distributed across people and places, and in the household decisions that determine who takes up new technologies.</p>

<p>My <a href="/research/">job market paper</a> studies how the organization of the supply chain shapes the benefits of a targeted subsidy. Electric vehicles are unusual in that they are sold both through franchised dealers and directly by manufacturers, and I use the institutional design of a targeted EV subsidy program, together with transaction-level vehicle registration records, to separate the effects of dealer intermediation from those of the subsidy itself.</p>

<p>This work is supported by the Wilkes Dissertation Fellowship from the <a href="https://wilkescenter.utah.edu/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Wilkes Center for Climate Science and Policy, University of Utah</a>, and will be presented at the Wilkes Climate Summit in 2027.</p>

<p>In the classroom, I have taught microeconomics, public policy, econometrics and statistics, and mathematics for economists at the graduate and undergraduate levels.</p>
